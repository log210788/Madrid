## 🏛️ STAGE 1: Spontaneous Rhetoric — "The 16-Second Presidential Pitch" (00:00 – 06:30)

### 🎯 Pedagogical Objective
Train rapid verbal synthesis under strict countdown pressure. The objective was to eliminate hesitation, formulate an opening thesis immediately, and anchor persuasive arguments in tangible civic benefits.

### 🎙️ The Game Setup & Spoken Dialogue
Louie opened with a rapid challenge: Diego had **16 seconds** to convince voters why he should be elected President.

* **Diego's Platform:**
  - *"I think I'd be a good president because I will invest in the quality of life of people."*
  - *"Believe in new houses and gardens where everyone can walk calm."*
  - *"The best option for my candidacy is to reduce poverty and in a short time increase salaries."*
* **The Economic Cross-Examination:**
  - Louie challenged the mechanics: *"How will you raise salaries?"*
  - Diego playfully replied: *"Printing more money! It's the easiest way."*
  - Louie and Diego explored the economic consequences: inflation, public spending deficits, and immigration pressures across Southern Europe and Spain.

### 🧠 Grammatical & Stylistic Takeaways
1. **Candidacy vs. Candidature:** In Spanish, *candidatura* naturally suggests *candidature*, but in modern English political rhetoric, **candidacy** is the primary noun (*"the focus of my candidacy"*).
2. **Poverty vs. The Poor:** Frame economic policy around the abstract condition: *"alleviate / reduce poverty"*, rather than *"reduce the poor"*.
3. **Time Expressions:** Duration is uncountable: use *"in the near future"* or *"in a short time"*, never *"in a few time"*.

## 🛠️ Gentle Phrasing Polishes: 6 Supportive Upgrades

| Timestamp | Spoken in the Moment | Natural Executive Polish | Linguistic & Grammatical Rationale |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **🕒 01:46** | *"The best option for my candidature is to reduce the poor"* | **"The core focus of my candidacy is to alleviate poverty"** | In English, *candidatura* is **candidacy** (or *campaign*). Alleviate / reduce *poverty* (the condition) rather than *the poor* (the people). |
| **🕒 03:01** | *"Maybe in a few time, we can increase the salaries"* | **"In the near future / Soon, we will be able to raise salaries"** | *Time* as duration is uncountable: say *"in a short time"* or *"in the near future"*. In economics, *"raise salaries"* is standard. |
| **🕒 04:44** | *"People can came... they can't came easily"* | **"People can come... they cannot come easily"** | Modal auxiliary verbs (*can, could, should, must, will*) must always be followed by the bare base infinitive (*come*), never the past form (*came*). |
| **🕒 27:15** | *"I trust on you in that story"* | **"I trust you on that story / I believe your account"** | The verb *trust* takes a direct personal object (*I trust you*). Anchor specific statements or claims with *on* (*I trust you on that*). |
| **🕒 51:00** | *"I have too much CV that the others... only for work abroad"* | **"I have a much stronger CV than the others... just by working abroad"** | *Too much* denotes an excessive negative defect. For positive superiority, use *a much stronger CV / far more experience*. Prepositions require the gerund (*by working*). |
| **🕒 59:16** | *"I don't know how you can to live there... I probably be in panic"* | **"I don't know how you can live there... I would probably panic"** | Omit *to* after modal *can* (*how you can live*). Use the conditional auxiliary *would* to express hypothetical reactions (*I would probably panic*). |
